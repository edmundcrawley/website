Appointment in Crete — facsimile reprint: assessment and plan

A. M. Rendel, Appointment in Crete: The Story of a British Agent. London: Allan Wingate, 1953. Printed by Page Bros. (Norwich) Ltd. 240 pp. Dust jacket by John Craxton.

Goal: a print-ready replica of the 1953 first edition (same page size, typeface, layout, line and page breaks, map endpaper, dust jacket) with a fully corrected text, so that Clare can have bound copies made for the grandchildren.

1. What we have, and what is wrong with it

1.1 The PDF in the repo (the good news)

  • It is a 600 dpi black-and-white scan of every page, with an OCR text layer added by ABBYY FineReader 11. The scan itself is excellent: sharp, clean, high enough resolution to measure the original typography to a fraction of a point and to trace the chapter ornament and the map.
  • 246 scanned pages = the map endpaper (p.1 of the PDF), four blank pages, title, copyright page, contents, dedication, text pages 7–240, then three blanks.
  • Every chapter opens on the page the Contents table says it does, so no leaves are missing from the text block.

1.2 The OCR text (the problem)

The Word file and the Kindle (.mobi) file are both just exports of the same ABBYY text, so all three carry the same errors. The Word file has zero margins on US Letter paper with one Word “section” per page and no usable formatting; the .mobi has the same text plus a low-resolution copy of the map. Neither is salvageable as a layout; only the text is worth keeping as one of the inputs.

1.4 Research findings (see reference/RESEARCH.md for sources)

  • The dust jacket was designed by John Craxton, the painter who did Patrick Leigh Fermor’s jackets. Front: two figures by a tree in blue, yellow and black. Spine: title and author in white on blue. Back panel: two photographs of the author captioned British Officer and Cretan Partisan, a biography headed A. M. RENDEL, and the Allan Wingate address. Front flap: the blurb, “Jacket design by John Craxton”, “15s. net”. Back flap: the blurb continues (“continued on back flap”).
  • Low-resolution photographs of the jacket (front, back, flap, spine) exist on booksellers’ sites and are saved under reference/web/. They show the whole design and most of the text is legible, but at 500–600 pixels they are not printable. A proper scan of a real jacket is needed (§4).
  • The photographs of your grandfather “in his shepherd clothes as well as his civilian clothes” are the two jacket-back photographs. They do not appear inside the book. Nobody online has a better copy than the jacket itself, so the jacket scan is also the source for these.
  • Bindings vary: brown/maroon cloth with gilt spine in jacketed copies photographed by dealers; green cloth with “APPOINTMENT IN CRETE — RENDEL” in gilt up the spine on an ex-library copy.
  • Contemporary review: The Spectator, 25 September 1953, “Cretan Labyrinth” (the reviewer liked the book and disliked the jacket). Full text saved in reference/RESEARCH.md. Could be printed as an insert or an afterword if the family wants it; I would not put it in the facsimile itself.
  • First editions in the Craxton jacket are now selling for US$2,000–3,800, so this is a book worth doing properly.

2. Approach

The scan tells us exactly where every line of the original breaks and where every page ends. Rather than re-flowing the text and hoping the page count comes out the same, I will typeset the book line for line: every page of the new book will carry the same words on the same lines as the 1953 printing, justified to the same measure, at the same line pitch, in the same position on the page. Page 133 of the new book will be page 133 of the old one. I have already proved this works on two sample pages (reference/scan-samples/demo-*.png): overlaid on the scan, the new setting lands on top of the original to within a fraction of a millimetre.

Step 1 — Build the text master (no input needed from you)

  1. Extract the ABBYY text line by line, with each line’s position (which tells me paragraph starts, indents, italics, page numbers).
  2. Run a second, modern OCR engine (Tesseract 5, already run on all 246 pages at 600 dpi) over the same scans. Where the two engines agree the text is almost certainly right; where they disagree, flag it.
  3. Read every flagged line (and, as a safety net, every page) against the page image myself and correct it. Build a list of every proper name in the book (people, villages, mountains, Greek words) and check each against the scan.
  4. Produce text/master.json: one record per page, one per line, with paragraph/indent/italic flags, end-of-line hyphenation preserved, and a corrections log so anyone can see what was changed and why.

Editorial rule: reproduce the 1953 text exactly, including its spellings (e.g. “Herakleion”, “Mt. Ida”, “jackboots”), its punctuation and its printer’s conventions. Genuine 1953 misprints, if any turn up, go on a list for you to decide about rather than being silently fixed.

Step 2 — Typeset the replica

  • Page size, margins, 35-line grid, measure, folio positions and chapter-opening positions all taken from the scan measurements above.
  • Each line set as in the original and justified to the measure; last lines of paragraphs ranged left; poem and tapered ending set as in the original.
  • Drop capitals, small capitals, chapter ornament (traced from the 600 dpi scan as a vector), signature marks, “FINIS”.
  • Light “ink gain” (a hairline stroke on the letters) to match the heavier colour of letterpress printing, so the pages look like the original rather than like a laser print.
  • Output: a press-ready PDF (PDF/X-style, fonts embedded, 300+ dpi for any images) at the exact trim size, plus a version with 3 mm bleed/crop marks if the printer asks for it.
  • Map: cleaned up from the 600 dpi scan (despeckle, straighten, re-black), supplied as a separate single-sheet PDF at the endpaper size so it can be printed and pasted down as in the original, and also included as the first page of the book PDF.

Step 3 — Dust jacket

  • Reassemble the jacket from a scan of a real one (your aunt’s two halves stitched together, or Robert’s complete copy — see §4). Clean the paper tone, repair chips and tears digitally, keep Craxton’s colours.
  • Re-set the back panel biography and the flap text in type so they are crisp; re-use the two photographs from the best scan available.
  • Supply as a one-piece PDF laid out for the actual book thickness (the spine width depends on the paper the printer uses, so this is done last, once the printer tells us the paper).

Step 4 — Proofing

  • Automated overlay of every one of the 240 new pages on its scanned original, to catch any line that drifts.
  • A side-by-side proof PDF (scan on the left, new page on the right) for you and Clare to spot-check.
  • Final read-through of the text master against the scans.

Step 5 — Extras (cheap once the text is clean)

  • A clean, reflowable EPUB/Kindle file for anyone who wants to read it on a device.
  • A corrected Word document, replacing the broken one.

3. Decisions to make

Typeface. The original is Monotype Baskerville, which is a commercial font. Options: 1. Baskervville (open source, included in fonts/): a revival of an 18th-century Baskerville cut; very close letter shapes and it has the old-style figures the book uses. Slightly lighter and narrower than Monotype’s version, which the ink-gain trick and a half-point size increase compensate for. This is my default and costs nothing. 2. Libre Baskerville (open source, included): darker and closer in weight to the printed page, but a larger x-height and lining figures, so page numbers and dates (1943) look modern. 3. Buy Monotype Baskerville (Regular + Italic, roughly £80–£120 as a desktop licence): the actual typeface. If you want the replica to be as exact as it can be, this is the one thing money can buy. If you have a Mac, Apple ships a Monotype-licensed Baskerville with macOS, which you could compare against the samples.

Compare the two free options in reference/scan-samples/demo-side-by-side-*.png (scan on the left, new setting on the right) and tell me which you prefer, or whether to buy the Monotype face.

Signature marks. The “2—A.I.C.” marks at the foot of pp. 17, 33, 49… are a printer’s artefact. My default is to keep them, since the brief is an identical replica; say if you would rather drop them.

Prelims. If your copy has a half-title (a page before the title page with just “APPOINTMENT IN CRETE”), I will add it. The scan has a blank there.

8. What still needs the real book, and what I am unsure of

Dust jacket (the main one). The reconstruction is built from photographs of 560–800 pixels. The front panel is sharp because the design is three flat colours and could be separated cleanly, but fine hatching is simplified and the colours are my best guess at the printed inks (ultramarine, pale yellow ochre, black on cream). The spine is the weakest part: both photographs show it at an angle with the lettering cut off, so it has been re-lettered in a plain bold sans rather than Craxton’s hand lettering. The two photographs of your grandfather on the back are upscaled from about 100 × 150 pixels and are soft. The back flap text (the continuation of the blurb) is nowhere online, so the back flap currently carries a bracketed note and a short “About this edition” paragraph. A flat 600 dpi scan of Clare’s or Robert’s jacket replaces all of this in an afternoon.

Trim size and margins. Taken from the scan (130.5 × 207 mm). A scanner crops a few millimetres, so the true page is probably 1–3 mm larger. Please measure a page and the boards (PLAN §4 item 1); the files are re-generated at the exact size in minutes.

Half-title. The scan has blank leaves where pages 1–2 would be. I have included a half-title reading APPOINTMENT IN CRETE, as every book of this kind had one. Check the family copy: if there is no half-title, or it reads differently, it is a one-line change.

Map placement. Dealers describe the maps on the front pastedown in some copies and on the free endpaper in others. Tell me where it is in your copy and whether the back endpaper also carries it.

Typeface. Baskervville is a free revival of an eighteenth-century Baskerville and sits a little lighter and narrower than the Monotype Baskerville of the original; I have compensated with size and ink spread, and the line breaks and page breaks are the original’s. If the family would like the exact face, Monotype Baskerville (Regular and Italic) costs roughly £80–120 as a desktop licence and drops straight in.

Spelling and punctuation. The text reproduces the 1953 printing, including its 33 misprints and inconsistencies (for example “Herakleion” and “Heraklion”, “Vassili” and “Vasili”, “pigeon Greek”, “witner” for winter), all listed in text/CORRECTIONS.md. Say if you would rather have the obvious misprints silently corrected; it is a five-minute change either way.

Three judgement calls in the proofreading are flagged in the log and worth a glance against the book: p. 19 “had to help” (a faint dot after “had” judged to be a speck, not a full stop); p. 144 “of” (a broken letter); p. 163 a faint comma after “brightly”.

Spine width. Set at 22 mm for the jacket, which suits 240 pages on 80–90 gsm cream paper; the printer’s figure will replace it.

Signature marks (“2—A.I.C.” … “15—A.I.C.”) have been kept as in the original. Say if you would rather lose them.

6. Kindle edition and how to share it

What I will make. A proper EPUB 3 e-book built from the corrected text master (not from the Word file): real chapter structure and table of contents, the two maps as a sharp image at the front, a cover (the Craxton jacket front once we have a good scan; a typographic cover until then), italics and the verse preserved, the title page, copyright page and dedication reproduced, and a short note at the end recording that this is a family reprint of the 1953 edition. It will be validated with the standard EPUB checker and tested in Kindle’s own previewer so it renders cleanly on e-ink Kindles, the Kindle apps and Fire tablets. The old .mobi format is obsolete: Amazon stopped accepting it for Send to Kindle in 2022 and converts EPUB to its own format automatically, so EPUB is the right file to distribute.

The easiest way onto a Kindle. Amazon’s “Send to Kindle” is built for exactly this. Each family member needs to do one of these, once: 1. Go to amazon.co.uk/sendtokindle in a web browser, sign in with the Amazon account the Kindle is registered to, and drag the EPUB file onto the page. It appears on their Kindle within a minute or two over Wi-Fi. 2. Or, on a phone or tablet with the Kindle app installed, open the EPUB attachment from email or WhatsApp and choose Share → Kindle. 3. Or email the EPUB as an attachment to their personal @kindle.com address (found under Manage Your Content and Devices → Preferences → Personal Document Settings; the sender’s email must be on their approved list).

So distribution is simply: I give you the EPUB, you send it to the family by email or WhatsApp, and each person does step 1 or 2. The file will be about 1 MB. Books sent this way live in the person’s Kindle library under “Docs” and sync position, notes and highlights across their devices like any purchased book.

What does not work: Amazon’s Family Library sharing does not cover personal documents, and there is no way to put a private book in the Kindle Store without publishing it for sale to the public, which we do not want for a family copy of a book that may still be in copyright. (Rendel died in 1991, so the text is in copyright in the UK until 2061; a private family reprint of your grandfather’s book is a different matter from selling it, but it is worth knowing.)

I will also supply the same book as a PDF of the facsimile for anyone who prefers to see the real pages on a tablet.

7. Printing options in the UK

What we are asking for is a short run (10–50 copies) of a 240-page black-and-white hardback at a custom trim of about 130 × 207 mm, ideally in cloth with foil lettering on the spine, a printed map endpaper and a full-colour dust jacket. That is a normal job for the UK’s short-run and digital book printers, who serve small publishers and self-publishers every day, and most will take a print-ready PDF from an individual. Prices below are indicative, from printers’ published calculators and price sheets (October 2026 unless noted); a full cloth-and-jacket specification is “quote only” everywhere, so the next step is to send the same specification to two or three of them and compare.

7.1 The specification to send for quotes

  • Trim 130 × 207 mm (or A5 148 × 210 if the printer cannot do custom; the facsimile can be imposed on A5 with slightly wider margins). 240 pp + 2 pp prelims if a half-title is added.
  • Text: black only, on 80–90 gsm cream book paper (Bookwove Cream or Munken Pure / Enso Cream are the usual names).
  • Binding: sewn or PUR-glued case; cloth (Wibalin Buckram or similar) in a colour to match the family copy; gold foil on the spine (“APPOINTMENT IN CRETE — RENDEL”); printed endpapers (the maps, black on cream) front and plain back, or the map on both.
  • Dust jacket: full colour on 150–170 gsm uncoated or silk paper, matt laminated or unlaminated, flaps 70–80 mm.
  • Quantities: 10, 20 and 50. Ask for a printed proof copy before the run.

Jacket details that matter for the file I prepare: every platform wants the jacket as one single-page PDF (back flap, back, spine, front, front flap) built to the spine width for their paper. Separate-jacket route (plain cloth book from one supplier, jacket printed elsewhere): a 130 × 207 mm jacket with 80 mm flaps is about 480 × 215 mm flat, too big for SRA3 digital sheets unless the flaps are cut to 65 mm. Only worth it if the chosen book printer cannot do jackets.
