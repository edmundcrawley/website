Making it an heirloom

The facsimile reproduces the book. What would turn it into a gift that a grandchild in 2066 is glad to own is the material only the family has: a few pages in Clare’s voice, the photographs in the drawer, a who’s who for readers who were not alive in 1943. These are ideas, not a list of chores; the book can be printed exactly as it stands. Please pass this page to anyone in the family, or among Sandy’s friends, who might have thoughts or material.

If only three things were added, I would choose these. They cost little, need nobody’s permission, and are what the grandchildren will value most.

1. A family foreword

Two or three pages by Clare, and perhaps her brothers and sisters, about their father: what he was like at home, whether he talked about Crete, the Cretan friends who came to stay.

2. Photographs

Sandy in Crete or just after; the two jacket portraits in their original prints; Elizabeth, to whom the book is dedicated; his Cretan companions; the cave above Katharo.

3. A who’s who

One page naming the people the book takes for granted: Tom Dunbabin, Paddy Leigh Fermor, Stanley Moss, Xan Fielding, the kapetans, Rosebud and Clubfoot, Friar Tuck.

Everything new would be set in the same type as the book, placed outside the 1953 text (before it as a foreword, after it as an afterword and appendix) and clearly marked as an addition, so that the book itself stays exactly the book.

A family foreword

A facsimile says what the book was; a foreword says why it is being given. It need not be long. Two or three pages in the voice of someone who knew him are worth more than any review: what he was like at the breakfast table, whether the war came up, the shepherd’s cloak if it survived, the Greek friends, the holidays in Crete if there were any, what he would have made of his grandchildren reading it.

Possible shapes: a letter from Clare to the grandchildren; a few paragraphs from each of his children; or a single short piece with a line of memory from each family member. I can edit, typeset and fit whatever comes, and I am happy to draft a separate editorial note (what this edition is, how it was made, and that the misprints are the 1953 printer’s) so that the family pages can be purely personal.

Where it goes: before the 1953 title page feels right for a gift, so the first thing opened is the family’s voice; after FINIS keeps the original opening untouched. Either works; the type and paper will be the same.

Material only the family has

None of this is on the internet, and all of it would make the book richer. Worth asking round the family for:

Photographs

  • Sandy in Crete or in Cairo, in uniform or in his shepherd’s clothes. Wes Davis’s book The Ariadne Objective (2013) credits photographs to the “Rendel Family Archive”, so some exist, and whoever lent them knows where they are.
  • The two jacket portraits (“British Officer”, “Cretan Partisan”) as original prints, which would replace the soft copies made from a photograph of the jacket.
  • Elizabeth, and the two of them together: the book is dedicated “To My Wife”.
  • His Cretan companions, named in the book (Mikhali, Vassili, Leftheri, Christo and others), and any reunion photographs from later visits.
  • The places: the cave above Katharo, now known locally as the “cave of the English”; Tapais; the monastery.

Papers

  • Letters home from Cairo and Crete, 1943–45; a page in his hand reproduced as a plate is worth a chapter of commentary.
  • His letters to Annette Crean, which Rick Stroud used in Kidnap in Crete and which are at the Imperial War Museum (reference 95/34/1); copies can be requested.
  • His SOE reports and signals, which are at the National Archives at Kew in the HS 5 series (Crete), and may be photographed there.
  • The M.B.E. citation; later articles he wrote in The Times about Crete; correspondence with Leigh Fermor or Dunbabin.
  • Anything from Allan Wingate: the contract, royalty statements or letters. These would also settle who holds the copyright today.

Objects

  • The sariki (black headscarf), the shepherd’s cloak, the knife, the wireless crystal, the medals, the tin the cyanide pills came in. A single photograph of such things laid on a plain cloth makes a lovely plate.

The book itself

  • Inscribed copies. A copy inscribed “To Mrs Henson, with the author’s respectful compliments, 14.7.53” was sold recently; family copies may carry inscriptions worth reproducing in his hand.
  • The review cuttings he surely kept, which may include the Times and TLS reviews we could not read online.
  • The dust jacket, flat and complete, which fixes the jacket for good.

Memories

  • A few lines from each of his children and from anyone who knew him; whether any grandchild has been to Tapais or Katharo; the Cretan families, if the connection survives.

Helping a reader born this century

The book assumes a reader who lived through the war. Four modest additions, each a page or two, would let a grandchild read it without a search engine beside them:

  • A who’s who, half a dozen lines on each of the people the book takes for granted, and on the Cretan names the author uses.
  • A timeline of Sandy’s war set against the island’s: his landing in September 1943, the kidnapping of General Kreipe in April 1944, the German surrender in May 1945, with the dates of the book’s chapters.
  • A modern map of eastern Crete with today’s spellings beside the book’s (Herakleion and Iraklio, Neapolis, Tapais, Katharo), so the places can be found on a holiday. It could sit on the back endpaper, mirroring the 1953 map at the front.
  • What happened next: his twenty-three years as diplomatic correspondent of The Times, the C.B.E., the Cretan friends he kept, the cave that still bears the name of the English.

The reviews of 1953 and the later histories that draw on the book are already gathered on the reviews page and in the optional appendix.

Touches for the printed book

  • The real typeface. Monotype Baskerville, the face of the original, can be bought for about £80–120 and dropped in; it is the one purchase that makes the type exact.
  • A limitation page: “Printed for the family of A. M. Rendel in an edition of twenty-five copies, of which this is number __, for __________”, with the recipient’s name written in. Grandchildren keep a book with their name in it.
  • A frontispiece. A photograph of Sandy facing the title page, as memoirs of the 1950s often had and this one lacked.
  • The back endpaper, blank in the original, used for the modern map or a family photograph.
  • Binding to match the family copy: cloth colour, gilt spine lettering, and a ribbon marker in the jacket’s blue.
  • A slipcase for one or two presentation copies, if any are hand-bound.

The Kindle edition and this website

Whatever is added to the book can go into the Kindle edition too. This website could become the family’s archive for the book: a page of photographs and documents at full size as they turn up, the scans of the reviews already here, and a standing invitation for anyone with memories or papers to send them. It costs nothing to keep, and it reaches the cousins who will never own a printed copy.

How to send things

  • Photographs and documents: a flatbed scan at 600 dpi is best; a phone photograph straight on, in daylight, with the item flat, is a good second best. Send the original-size file, not a compressed copy from a messaging app.
  • Memories: an email of a few lines is enough; I will do the editing and typesetting. Longer is welcome.
  • Objects: a photograph on a plain cloth in daylight.
  • Where: to Edmund, at edmundcrawley@gmail.com. Please forward this page to anyone who might have something, or thoughts about what should go in.

Two cautions, so that the gift stays a book and not a scrapbook: the 1953 text itself stays untouched, with everything new outside it and labelled; and additions of sixteen to thirty-two pages keep the right proportion against the original’s 240.
